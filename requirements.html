IE 421 · Data Science for Engineers · Istanbul Bilgi University

Term Project: tell a story with data

Pick a theme, take real data, clean it, explore it, visualize it well and publish a data story on GitHub Pages. No predictive model is required: the project is about data handling, exploratory analysis, visualization and storytelling.

40% of the course gradeTeams of 3–47 themes · 2 teams per themeTeam sign-up sheet · closes Wed 14 Oct, 17:00

1What you will build

A public repository inside the course organization BILGI-IE-421 and a GitHub Pages website that contains:

  1. A data card for every dataset: source, license, time range, size, columns, known issues, cleaning decisions.
  2. Data preparation code that joins at least two sources, parses dates correctly and reshapes data where needed (merge, groupby, pivot/melt).
  3. An exploratory analysis: first contact (types, sample, missing values, duplicates, identifiers), one variable at a time, then relationships.
  4. Four static visualizations that each answer part of a research question: a distribution, a relationship, a trend over time and a comparison across categories.
  5. One interactive visualization (bokeh or plotly) embedded in the page, with limited and obvious interaction.
  6. One advanced visualization: a map (folium), an animation, a 3D plot or a network, chosen because it carries the argument, not as decoration.
  7. A data story: active titles, one-sentence takeaways under every figure, numbers in context, a conclusion that takes a side, limitations and ethics.
Not required: regression, classification or any predictive model. If you include one it is not graded. Spend the time on data quality, chart design and narrative.
Data: use at least one dataset from your theme's list in Section 3. You may add other datasets; list every dataset in your proposal and the instructor confirms them with the proposal feedback.

3Themes and datasets

Seven themes, each built around data that is rich enough for maps, animations, networks and long time series. Links were checked on 9 October 2026; Kaggle datasets need a free account. Weather features for any theme: Open-Meteo Historical Weather API (no key). Raw files over 50 MB must not be committed: commit a download script and a sample or an aggregated file.

T1 · cap 2 teams

Restless Earth: earthquakes, storms, fires

  • USGS earthquake catalog (CSV via the FDSN API: magnitude, depth, location, 1900 to today)
  • NOAA IBTrACS tropical cyclone tracks (CSV, 1842 to today)
  • NASA FIRMS active fire detections (archive download or API, free key)
  • NOAA significant volcanic eruptions · NASA meteorite landings

Story ideas: the heavy tail of magnitudes, storm tracks animated by decade, fire seasons on a map, where meteorites fall versus where people look for them.

T2 · cap 2 teams

People on the Move: migration and refugees

  • UNHCR Refugee Data Finder API (population by year, origin and asylum country; Kaggle mirror)
  • UN DESA International Migrant Stock (origin–destination matrix)
  • KNOMAD remittance flows (World Bank)
  • Eurostat asylum applications · UN World Population Prospects

Story ideas: flows as Sankey or chord diagrams, who hosts and who leaves, a decade of displacement, remittances compared with aid.

T3 · cap 2 teams

The World at Play: football and Formula 1

  • International football results 1872 to today (Kaggle, every national-team match)
  • FIFA World Cup matches (Kaggle)
  • Formula 1 World Championship 1950 to today (Kaggle: races, laps, pit stops, standings)
  • Club football datasets (GitHub collection)

Story ideas: 150 years of home advantage, eras of dominance, how pit stops got faster, goals per game across decades.

T4 · cap 2 teams

Culture by the Numbers: music, film, LEGO, Nobel, names

  • Top Spotify songs in 73 countries · Billboard Hot 100 since 1958 (Kaggle)
  • IMDb non-commercial datasets (TSV files at datasets.imdbws.com)
  • LEGO database (Rebrickable: sets, parts, colours, themes since 1950)
  • Nobel Prize API · US baby names since 1880 (Kaggle)

Story ideas: the colours of LEGO by decade, how pop went global, the age of a laureate, names that rose and fell.

T5 · cap 2 teams

Hot Planet: temperature, ice and CO2

  • NASA GISTEMP (global and zonal monthly anomalies)
  • NSIDC Sea Ice Index (daily extent since 1978)
  • NOAA Mauna Loa CO2 (the Keeling curve)
  • Daily temperature of major cities 1995–2020 · Berkeley Earth (Kaggle)

Story ideas: warming stripes, the climate spiral as an animation, the shrinking ice, city heat calendars.

T6 · cap 2 teams

Cities We Live In: homes and bike-share

  • Inside Airbnb (listings with location, price and reviews for 100+ cities)
  • Capital Bikeshare, Washington DC · TfL cycle hire, London · Divvy, Chicago
  • Paris bike counters

Story ideas: tourism's footprint by neighbourhood, the daily pulse of a bike-share system, price maps, two cities compared.

T7 · cap 2 teams

The Connected World: trade, flights and prices

  • UN Comtrade Plus (bilateral trade by product)
  • OpenFlights airports and routes · OpenSky COVID-19 flight dataset (Zenodo)
  • The Economist Big Mac index (GitHub)
  • World Bank internet users

Story ideas: trade as a network, the world's air routes on one map, the day the skies emptied, Big Mac purchasing power.

  • FiveThirtyEight data · The Pudding data
  • Information is Beautiful

Your repository starts from the demo repository (demo site). Keep this layout:

PROPOSAL.md        milestone 1: problem, datasets, questions (template provided)

requirements.txt   libraries you import
index.html         your GitHub Pages site (css/ allowed)
data/
  README.md        data card (template provided)
  *.csv            samples or aggregated data only, each under 50 MB
scripts/
  download_data.py fetches raw data from the source
  preprocess.py    cleaning, joins, date parsing, reshaping
  eda.ipynb        exploratory analysis
  figures.py       produces every figure in visuals/
visuals/
  *.png, *.html    every figure used on the site (interactive figures as HTML)

A GitHub Actions check named structure-check runs on every push. It flags missing files, an unfilled README, data files over 50 MB, hard-coded local paths such as /content/drive, and scripts that do not compile. Keep it green; it is part of the repository score.

6Requirements in detail

6.1 Data preparation

  • Join at least two sources. Reading one of them from an API or JSON is a plus.
  • Parse dates explicitly and consistently (one helper function, one dayfirst setting). Document time zones and units.
  • Handle missing values, duplicates and identifier columns deliberately and write the decision in the data card.
  • Use groupby, pivot or melt where the question needs it; do not pre-aggregate in Excel by hand.

6.2 Exploratory analysis

  • First contact: column types, a random sample, missing values and duplicates, what the identifiers are.
  • One variable at a time: summary statistics, histograms or box plots, counts of categories, values over time.
  • Relationships: correlation heatmap or scatter matrix, group comparisons. Write what you found, not only what you plotted.

6.3 Static figures

  • Four figures: a distribution, a relationship, a trend over time, a comparison across categories. Each answers part of a research question.
  • Label axes with units; put the data source under every figure.
  • Bar charts start at zero and are ordered meaningfully. No pie charts. Line charts carry at most four lines, labelled directly.
  • Use a logarithmic axis for heavy-tailed data; show uncertainty (error bars or confidence bands) when you compare averages.
  • Use a colour-blind-safe palette and colour strategically to point at the message.

6.4 Interactive and advanced figures

  • Interactive: bokeh or plotly, exported to HTML and embedded in the page. Interaction is limited to what helps the reader (hover, filter, select); controls are obvious.
  • Advanced: a folium map or choropleth, an animation, a 3D plot or a network. It must carry an argument that a static chart could not.

6.5 The data story

  • Structure: an opening hook, the three questions, findings in order, a conclusion that takes a side, limitations and ethics, references.
  • Active titles that state the finding ("Delays cluster in the evening", not "Delay by hour").
  • One-sentence takeaway under every figure; annotate the important numbers on the figure itself.
  • Numbers in context with sensible significant digits. Answer "so what?" for your audience.
  • One language throughout the site and the repository.

8Policies

Individual contribution

  • Every member must have meaningful commits in all milestones. The commit history is read when grading.
  • Peer evaluation closes on 11 December: each member rates teammates from 1 to 5 with a one-sentence justification.
  • A member with no commits and a peer rating below 2 can lose up to 50% of the team score.

AI tools

  • You may use AI assistants (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot and others).
  • Record every use in AI_USAGE.md: tool, task, where it is used, what you verified and how.
  • Be able to explain every line of code and every figure. During presentations each member is asked about code or results; a member who cannot explain their part loses presentation points.
  • The data card, limitations and conclusions are written in your own words. Generated text in these sections must be declared.
  • Undeclared use is an academic integrity violation under the University policy.
